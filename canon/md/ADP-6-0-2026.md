###### **Preface**

ADP 6-0, _Command and Control_, provides doctrine on the fundamentals of command and control and the
command and control warfighting function. Additionally, it describes the mission command approach to
command and control. Through this approach, commanders combine the art and science of command and
control to understand situations, make decisions, direct actions, and lead forces toward mission
accomplishment.

Applicable to all members of the Army profession, the principal audience for ADP 6-0 is Army commanders,
leaders, and unit staffs. It applies to Army forces during the conduct of operations and to Army forces
performing duties at a home station. ADP 6-0 provides the foundation for training and Army education
system curricula concerning command and control of Army forces.

To comprehend ADP 6-0, readers should understand the fundamentals of multidomain operations described
in ADP 3-0 and the fundamentals of leadership described in ADP 6-22. As the operations process is the
framework for exercising command and control, readers must also understand the fundamentals of the
operations process established in ADP 5-0. Details on the command and control system are found in FM 60.

ADP 6-0 implements North Atlantic Treaty Organization standardization agreement 2199. The Army
historically fights with joint and multinational partners as part of a coalition, and ADP 6-0 is nested with joint
and multinational doctrine. Army headquarters serving as a joint task force or multinational headquarters
should also refer to applicable joint or multinational doctrine concerning command and control of joint or
multinational forces.

Commanders, staffs, and subordinates ensure their decisions and actions comply with applicable U.S.,
international, and host nation laws and regulations when applying this doctrine. Commanders at all levels
ensure their Soldiers operate in accordance with the law of armed conflict and the rules of engagement as
discussed in FM 6-27/ MCTP 11-10C. They also adhere to the Army ethic described in ADP 6-22.

ADP 6-0 uses joint terms where applicable. Selected joint and Army terms and definitions appear in both the

###### **Introduction**

Command and control is fundamental to the art and science of warfare. It is the exercise of authority and
direction by a properly designated commander over assigned and attached forces. No single specialized
military function, either by itself or combined with others, has a purpose without it. Commanders are
responsible for command and control. Through command and control, commanders provide purpose and
direction to integrate all military activities towards a common goal—mission accomplishment.

The nature of war and the patterns of military history, however, show that war is not predictable or orderly.
War is both a contest of wills and a contest of intellect between two or more sides in conflict, with each trying
to alter the behavior of the other side. The complexity of friendly and enemy organizations, unique
combinations of terrain and weather, and the dynamic interaction among all participants create uncertainty.
Chance and friction increase the potential for chaos during operations.

As the Army’s approach to command and control, mission command empowers subordinate decision making
and decentralized execution appropriate to the situation. Mission command focuses on the objective of an
operation, not on how to achieve it. It emphasizes timely decision making, understanding of the higher
commander’s intent, and the clear responsibility of subordinates to act within that intent to achieve the desired
end state. With the commander’s intent to provide unity of effort, mission command relies on decentralized
execution and the initiative of subordinates.

Mission command requires tactically and technically competent commanders, staffs, and subordinate leaders
operating in an environment of mutual trust and shared understanding. It requires building effective teams
and a command climate in which commanders encourage subordinates to make decisions at the appropriate
level and exercise disciplined initiative to seize opportunities and counter threats within the commander’s
intent. Through mission orders, commanders focus their subordinates on the purpose of an operation rather
than on the details of how to perform assigned tasks. This allows subordinates the greatest possible freedom
of action in the context of a particular situation. When delegating authority to subordinates, commanders set
the necessary conditions for success by allocating resources and describing levels of risk tolerance to
subordinates based on assigned tasks.

Aspects of mission command—including commander’s intent, mutual trust, disciplined initiative, and
mission orders—have long been part of U.S. Army culture. General Grant’s orders to Sherman for the
campaign of 1864 and Sherman’s supporting plan are models of clear commander’s intent, mission orders,
and understanding based on mutual trust (See vignette on page 11). A more recent example is the 3rd Infantry
Division’s march to Baghdad in 2003 and the subsequent “thunder runs” that showed the world that the Iraqi
regime was defeated. Thunder Run was successful because the corps and division-level commanders
established clear intent in their orders and trusted their subordinates’ judgment and abilities to exercise
disciplined initiative in response to a fluid, complex problem, underwriting the risks that they took.

The Army codified the term mission command and its principles in the 2003 edition of FM 6-0. As the
Army’s first manual dedicated to command and control, FM 6-0 addressed the fundamentals of command
and control and described mission command as the Army’s preferred concept for exercising command and
control.

The 2012 edition of ADP 6-0 significantly revised Army command and control doctrine. It rescinded the
term command and control for Army forces and replaced it with mission command. The term mission
command became an activity, a warfighting function, a system, and a philosophy. The use of the term mission
command to describe multiple things created ambiguity. Mission command replaced command and control,
but in practical application it meant the same thing. This led to differing expectations among leadership
cohorts regarding the appropriate application of mission command during operations and garrison activities.
Labeling multiple things mission command eroded the importance of mission command as the approach to
exercising command and control.

**<u>Introduction</u>**


The 2019 edition of ADP 6-0 realigned Army command and control doctrine with joint, other service, and
allied doctrine that use the term command and control. Like the 2003 edition of FM 6-0, the 2019 edition of
ADP 6-0 once again described the fundamentals of command and control and defined mission command as
the Army approach for its exercise.

This version of ADP 6-0 represents key updates to the 2019 edition as it relates to the current operating
environment. Due to the vulnerabilities of large, static headquarters on the modern battlefield, command
posts are no longer the singular center point of command and control. In order to remain survivable,
commanders organize and operate their command and control system in a dispersed manner through a series
of command and control nodes. Other key updates and changes to this version of ADP 6-0 include—

    - Incorporating the Army’s operational concept of multidomain operations and related doctrine
established in FM 3-0.

    - Accounting for technical advancements that enhance friendly command and control.

    - Accounting for threat capabilities that can affect friendly command and control.

    - Incorporating the role of technology and data in supporting the science of control and the command
and control system.

    - Adjustments to the command and control system to include people, processes, data and networks,
and command and control nodes.

**THE NATURE OF WAR**



1-1. Military operations fall along a competition continuum that spans
from competition to armed conflict. Violent power struggles in failed
states, along with foreign powers seeking to gain strategic advantage,
present challenges to the joint force. Army forces must be prepared to
meet these challenges across the entire range of military operations.

1-2. The range of military operations helps relate military activities and operations in scope and purpose
against the backdrop of the competition continuum. The potential range of military activities and operations
extends from military engagement, security cooperation, and deterrence, up through large-scale combat
operations. Whether countering terrorism as part of a limited contingency operation or defeating great powers
in large-scale combat, the nature of war is constant. War is—

  - Fought to achieve a political purpose.

  - A human endeavor.

  - Chaotic and uncertain.



**FOUGHT TO ACHIEVE A POLITICAL PURPOSE**

1-3. All U.S. military operations share a common purpose: to achieve or contribute to national policy
objectives. An objective directs every military operation towards a clearly defined, decisive, and attainable
goal. Objectives reinforce the proper relationship between military operations and policy. War is always
subordinate to policy and serves a political end. In conjunction with political leaders, military leaders develop
strategies to achieve the desired policy outcomes. Policy outcomes often relate to the nation's ability to
influence, control, or secure populations, civil infrastructure, natural resources, and access to global commons
in all domains.

**HUMAN ENDEAVOR**

1-4. War is a human endeavor—a clash of wills characterized by the threat or application of force and
violence, often fought among populations. It is not a mechanical process that can be precisely controlled by
machines, calculations, or processes. It is not conducted in carefully controlled and predictable environments.
Fundamentally, all war is about changing human behavior. During operations, Army forces face adaptive
enemies while managing the will of its force and perception from inside and outside of an assigned area. As
friendly forces seek to impose their will on enemy forces, enemy forces resist and seek to impose their will
on friendly forces. A similar dynamic occurs among civilian groups whose own desires influence and are
influenced by military operations. All sides act, react, learn, and adapt. Appreciating these relationships is
essential to understanding the fundamental nature of war.

**CHAOTIC AND UNCERTAIN**

1-5. War, especially land combat, is inherently chaotic and uncertain. The complexity of friendly and enemy
organizations, unique combinations of terrain and weather, and the dynamic interaction among all
participants create uncertainty. Chance and friction, always present on the battlefield, further increase the
potential for chaos and uncertainty during operations. Chance refers to unexpected events or changes beyond
the control of friendly forces, while friction describes the challenges that make the execution of even simple
tasks difficult. Both are always present for all sides during combat.

1-6. The scale, scope, tempo, and lethality of large-scale combat operations intensify the dynamic and
uncertain nature of war, making precise cause-and-effect determinations difficult. The unintended effects of
operations often cannot be anticipated and may not be readily apparent. The nature of war demands an
approach to the C2 of operations that does not attempt to impose perfect order but rather makes allowances
for uncertainty created by chance and friction.

**ARMY OPERATIONS**

1-7. The primary responsibility of our Army is to conduct prompt and sustained land combat as part of the
joint force (ADP 1). Army forces accomplish their missions by conducting operations on behalf of a joint
force commander. An _operation_ is a sequence of tactical actions with a common purpose or unifying theme
(JP 1, Volume 1). The tenants and imperatives of multidomain operations guide Army forces during the
conduct of operations. _Multidomain operations_ are the combined arms employment of joint and Army
capabilities to create and exploit relative advantages to achieve objectives, defeat enemy forces, and
consolidate gains on behalf of joint force commanders (ADP 3-0).

1-8. Multidomain operations are the Army's contribution to joint campaigns during competition, crisis, and
armed conflict. Below the threshold of armed conflict, multidomain operations are how Army forces accrue
advantages and demonstrate readiness for conflict. This deters adversaries while assuring allies and partners.
During armed conflict, Army forces conduct multidomain operations to close with and destroy the enemy,
defeat enemy formations, seize critical terrain, and control populations and resources to deliver sustainable
political outcomes.

**RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN COMMAND AND CONTROL**

1-16. C2 are interrelated. Command resides with commanders. It includes the authority and responsibility
for effectively using available resources and for planning the employment of, organizing, directing,
coordinating, and controlling military forces for the accomplishment of missions. It also includes
responsibility for the health, welfare, morale, and discipline of assigned personnel. Command emphasizes a
commander's lawful assignment of authority and the responsibility that accompanies that authority.

1-17. Effective command is impossible without control. Control is inherent in command. It includes the
regulation of forces and warfighting functions. Commanders, supported by their staffs, control operations by
receiving and communicating information. This builds shared understanding and directs, coordinates, and
synchronizes the actions of subordinate units. Commander's intent, orders, control measures, and standard
operating procedures all assist with the control of operations. Determining the appropriate level of control in
a particular situation is a critical command responsibility.


**Command**

1-18. _Command_ is the authority that a commander in the armed forces lawfully exercises over subordinates
by virtue of rank or assignment (JP 1, Volume 2). Command is personal—an individual person commands,
not an organization or a headquarters. Command is considered more art than science, because it includes
intangible elements that require judgment in application. The elements of command are—

    - Authority.

    - Responsibility.

    - Decision making.

    - Leadership.

1-19. Inherent in command is the authority that a military commander lawfully exercises over subordinates,
including the authority to assign tasks and the responsibility for their successful completion. Authority
derives from two sources: official authority and personal authority. Official authority is a function of rank
and position, and it is bestowed by law and regulation. The authority to enforce orders by law is one of the
key elements of command, and it distinguishes military commanders from other leaders and managers.
Personal authority is a function of personal influence. It derives from factors such as experience, reputation,
skill, character, and personal example. It is bestowed by the other members of the organization. Official
authority provides the power to act, but it is rarely enough for success on its own. The most effective
commanders also possess a high degree of personal authority.

1-20. With authority comes responsibility. Commanders are legally and ethically responsible for their
decisions and for the actions, accomplishments, and failures of their subordinates. Commanders may delegate
authority, but delegation does not absolve commanders of their responsibility to the higher echelon
commander. Commanders are always accountable for what happens or fails to happen in their command.

1-21. Commanders exercise their authority through decision making. Decision making involves selecting the
most favorable course of action to accomplish the mission, including adjusting plans based on changing
circumstances. Effective decision making relies on data to help commanders and staffs develop
understanding and make informed decisions. Historically, commanders have leveraged advancements in
technology to gain advantages in decision making, a practice known as decision optimization. Current
decision optimization efforts are actively integrating artificial intelligence and advanced analytics to facilitate
faster, more informed decision making in today's dynamic environment. This integration enables units to
synthesize information and combine human and machine capabilities, providing the necessary capacity and
speed for decision making to achieve an operational advantage.

1-22. Decision making includes knowing when and what to decide while understanding the consequences of
those decisions. Commanders apply their understanding, visualization, description, and direction to make

**Control**

1-24. **_Control_** **is the regulation of forces and warfighting functions to accomplish the mission in**
**accordance with the commander’s intent** . Commanders exercise control over all forces in their assigned
area, aided by their C2 system (people, process, data and networks, and C2 nodes). Staff aid the commander
in exercising control by supporting the commander's decision making, assisting subordinate units, and
informing external organizations.

1-25. Control is more science than art because it relies on objectivity, empirical methods, and data driven
analysis. Control requires commanders and staffs to understand and assess the measurable aspects of
operations. These include the physical capabilities and limitations of friendly and enemy organizations and
systems. Control involves interpreting and applying data to evaluate operational conditions, monitor
progress, and adjust plans as needed. Control also requires a realistic appreciation for time and distance
factors and the time required to initiate and complete certain actions. Units are bound by factors such as
movement rates, supply consumption, weapons effects, and ethical and legal considerations. Computing and
communication technologies such as artificial intelligence, machine learning, and mesh networks
significantly contribute to effective C2. Commanders and staffs use these technologies to analyze vast
amounts of data and identify patterns, resulting in enhanced situational understanding and decision making.
The proper application of control incorporates some level of art, since commanders use judgment regarding
the abilities of subordinates and the likelihood that friction is part of every operation. The elements of control
are—

    - Information.

    - Communication.

    - Organization.

understanding, decision making, and directing actions across Information is central to all activities
all warfighting functions. Army forces rely on processing data Army forces undertake.
and analyzing information to understand situations and make
informed decisions. Staffs provide commanders with accurate and relevant information, which requires
access to reliable data and effective analysis. Commanders determine information requirements and set
information priorities. Equally important, commanders and staffs remain aware of the limitations, risks, and
potential threats associated with the information they rely on. Commanders and staffs identify, interpret, and
apply relevant information to make decisions.

1-27. Communication is the use of any means or method to convey information from one person or place to
another. Army forces employ to exercise control over forces by linking information and data to decisions and
decisions to action. Army leaders communicate data and information through networks to build and share a
common operational picture (COP), which enables coordinated action across the force. Commanders
anticipate those times when communication is likely to be intermittent, and they adjust their level of control
accordingly.

1-28. Commanders establish and maintain control through organization. Organizational considerations are
both internal (organizing the C2 system) and external (establishing command and support relationships
among subordinate forces). Commanders internally arrange the C2 system to synchronize activities, which
supports the commander's decision making and the effective conduct of operations. They task organize the
force through command and support relationships to weight the main effort and ensure forces are resourced
to accomplish assigned tasks. (See Chapter 3 for more information on control.)

**JOINT COMMAND AND CONTROL CONSIDERATIONS**

1-29. Army forces conduct operations as part of a joint force. A _joint force_ is a force composed of significant
elements, assigned or attached, of two or more Military Departments that operate under a single joint force
commander (JP 1, Volume 1). Army forces that are a direct subordinate unit to a joint force commander
utilize joint C2 doctrine, techniques, and procedures. Theater armies, Army forces headquarters, and corps
routinely interface with joint and other component headquarters throughout the conduct of operations. Key
joint C2 considerations include—

    - Joint command and support relationships (refer to JP 1, Volume 2).

      - Command relationships with the joint force commander.

      - Command relationships among components.

      - Functional component support and supporting relationships.

      - Command relationships with forces made available.

    - Joint task force headquarters organization and staff operations (refer to JP 3-33).

    - Joint planning (refer to JP 5-0).

    - Joint intelligence and intelligence, surveillance, and reconnaissance synchronization (refer to JP 20).

    - Joint operations in the information environment (refer to JP 3-04).

    - Joint communications (refer to JP 6-0).

    - Joint all-domain operations coordination and synchronization (refer to JP 3-0).

1-30. Within joint operations, Army forces employ capabilities from all domains (land, air, maritime, space,
and cyberspace). Each domain has a unique set of characteristics that influences how capabilities are
integrated and synchronized throughout a joint operation. Army forces understand the joint authorities,
processes, procedures, and time it takes to receive and assess effects from other domains and for Army forces
to create effects in those domains.

1-31. Army forces have effectively integrated capabilities and synchronized actions in the land, air, and
maritime domains for decades, and the authorities, processes, and procedures are well established. However,
the military application of technologies to space and cyberspace domains requires special consideration in
planning and execution. (Refer to JP 3-12 and JP 3-14 for more information on the military application in
space and cyberspace domains.)

**COMMAND AND CONTROL IN MULTINATIONAL ENVIRONMENTS**

1-32. _Multinational operations_ is a collective term to describe military actions conducted by forces of two or
more nations, usually undertaken within the structure of a coalition or alliance (JP 3-16). Multinational
operations are driven by a common agreement among the participating alliance or coalition partners. While
each nation has its own interests and participates within the limitations of national caveats, all nations bring

**MISSION COMMAND**

1-37. **_Mission command_** **is the Army’s approach to command and control that empowers subordinate**
**decision making and decentralized execution appropriate to the situation** . Mission command is based
on the Army's view that war is inherently chaotic and uncertain. No plan can account for every possibility,
and most plans change rapidly during execution to account for changes in the situation. No single person is
ever sufficiently informed to make every important decision, nor can a single person keep up with the number
of decisions that need to be made during combat. As such, mission command empowers subordinate leaders
to make decisions and act within the commander's intent to exploit opportunities and counter threats.

1-38. Subordinate leaders often have a better understanding of what is happening in their area than higher
echelon commanders have. They are more likely to respond effectively to threats and fleeting opportunities
if allowed to make decisions and act based on changing situations and unforeseen events. Enemy forces may
behave differently than expected, a route may become impassable, or units could consume supplies at
unexpected rates. Friction and unforeseeable combinations of variables impose uncertainty in all operations.
This requires an approach to C2 that does not attempt to impose perfect order but rather accepts uncertainty
and makes allowances for unpredictability.

1-39. Mission command capitalizes on subordinate ingenuity, innovation, and decision making to achieve
the commander's intent when conditions change or current orders are no longer relevant. It requires
subordinates who seek opportunities and commanders who accept risk for subordinates trying to meet their
intent. Subordinate decision making and decentralized execution appropriate to the situation help manage
uncertainty and tempo at each echelon during operations. Employing the mission command approach during
all garrison activities and training events is essential to creating the cultural foundation for its employment
in high-risk environments.

**SUBORDINATE DECISION MAKING**

1-40. Successful commanders anticipate future events by developing branches and sequels instead of
focusing on details better handled by subordinates during current operations. The higher the echelon, the
more time commanders devote to future operations and the broader the guidance provided to subordinates.
Subordinates empowered to make decisions during operations unburden higher commanders from issues that
distract from focus on broader perspective and critical issues.

1-41. Commanders delegate appropriate authority to deputies, subordinate commanders, and staff members
based on a judgment of their capabilities and experience. Delegation allows subordinates to decide and act
for their commander in specified areas. Delegating decision-making authority reduces the number of
decisions made at the higher echelons and the response time at lower echelons. In addition to determining
the amount of decision-making authority they will delegate, commanders also identify decisions that are their
sole responsibility and cannot be delegated to subordinates.

1-42. When delegating authority to subordinates, commanders strive to set the necessary conditions for
success. They do this by assessing and managing risk. Risk is inherent at all levels of command. Commanders
and staffs assess hazards and recommend controls to help manage risk, rather than forcing risk decisions on

1-43. While commanders can delegate authority, they cannot delegate responsibility. Subordinates are
accountable to their commanders for the use of delegated authority, but commanders remain solely
responsible and accountable for the actions of their subordinates.

**DECENTRALIZED EXECUTION**

1-44. Decentralized execution is the delegation of decision-making authority to subordinates, done so they
may make and implement decisions and adjust their assigned tasks in fluid and rapidly changing situations.
Decentralized execution is essential to seizing, retaining, and exploiting the initiative during operations in
environments where conditions rapidly change and uncertainty is the norm. Rapidly changing situations and
uncertainty are inherent in operations where commanders seek to establish a tempo and intensity that enemy
forces cannot match.

1-45. Decentralized execution requires disseminating information to the lowest possible level so subordinates
can make informed decisions based on a shared understanding of both the situation and their commander's
intent. This empowers subordinates operating in rapidly changing conditions to exercise disciplined initiative
within their commander's intent. It is the duty of subordinates to exercise initiative to achieve their
commander's intent. It is the commander's responsibility to issue appropriate intent and ensure subordinates
are prepared in terms of education, training, and experience to exercise initiative.

1-46. The commander's intent provides a unifying idea that allows decentralized execution within an
overarching framework. It provides guidance within which individuals exercise initiative to accomplish the
desired end state. Understanding the commander's intent two echelons up further enhances unity of effort
while providing the basis for decentralized decision making and execution throughout the depth of a
formation. Subordinates who understand the commander's intent are far more likely to exercise initiative
effectively in unexpected situations.

**LEVELS OF CONTROL**

1-47. Determining the appropriate level of control, including delegating authorities and determining how
much decentralized execution to employ, is an essential aspect of mission command. The level and
application of control is constantly evolving. It is continuously assessed and adjusted to ensure the level of
control is appropriate to the situation. Commanders allow subordinates the greatest freedom of action equal
to the level of acceptable risk in a particular situation. The mission variables (mission, enemy, terrain and
weather, troops and support available, time available, civil considerations, and informational considerations)
influence how much control to impose on subordinates. Other considerations include—

    - Enemy disposition and capabilities.

    - Level of integration and synchronization required.

    - Higher echelon headquarters constraints.

    - Level of risk.

    - Level of legal and ethical ambiguity.

    - Rules of engagement.

    - Level of unit cohesion.

    - Level of training.

    - Level of trust.

    - Level of shared understanding.

**PRINCIPLES OF MISSION COMMAND**

1-49. Mission command requires competent leaders and an environment of mutual trust and shared
understanding among commanders, staffs, and subordinates. It requires effective teams and a command
climate in which subordinates are required to seize opportunities and counter threats within the commander's
intent. Commanders issue mission orders that focus on the purpose of an operation and essential coordination
measures rather than the details of how to perform assigned tasks, giving subordinates the latitude to
accomplish those tasks in a manner that best fits the situation. This minimizes the number of decisions a
single commander makes and allows subordinates the greatest possible freedom of action to accomplish tasks.
Finally, when delegating authority to subordinates, commanders set the necessary conditions for success by
allocating appropriate resources to subordinates based on assigned tasks. Successful mission command is
enabled by the principles of—

    - Competence.

    - Mutual trust.

    - Shared understanding.

    - Commander's intent.

    - Mission orders.

    - Disciplined initiative.

    - Risk acceptance.

**SHARED UNDERSTANDING**

1-58. A critical challenge for commanders, staffs, and unified action partners is creating shared understanding

1-59. Shared understanding starts with the Army's doctrine and professional military education. These instill

**DISCIPLINED INITIATIVE**

1-77. Disciplined initiative refers to the duty individual subordinates have to exercise initiative within the
constraints of the commander's intent to achieve the desired end state. Simply put, disciplined initiative is
when subordinates have the discipline to follow their orders and adhere to the plan until they realize that their
orders and the plan are no longer suitable for the situation in which they find themselves. This may occur
because enemy forces do something unforeseen, there is a new or more serious threat, or a golden opportunity
emerges that offers a greater chance of success than the original course of action. The subordinate leader then
takes action on their own initiative to adjust to the new situation and achieve their commander's intent,
reporting to the commander about the new situation when able to do so.

1-78. Leaders and subordinates who exercise disciplined initiative create opportunities by taking action to
develop a situation without asking for further guidance. Commanders rely on subordinates to act to meet their
intent, not simply adhere to a plan that is no longer working. Under mission command, subordinates are
required to exercise disciplined initiative in the absence of orders, when current orders no longer apply, or
when an opportunity or threat presents itself. They, on their own responsibility, take actions that they think
will best accomplish the mission when communication with higher echelons is intermittent or decisions must
be made immediately.

1-79. A subordinate's initiative may be the starting point for seizing, retaining, and exploiting the operational
initiative by forcing an enemy to respond to friendly action. _Operational initiative_ is the setting of tempo and
terms of action throughout an operation (ADP 3-0). The effect of multiple subordinates exercising disciplined
initiative sets the conditions for friendly forces to seize the operational initiative in chaotic and ambiguous
situations.

1-80. When exercising initiative, neither commanders nor subordinates are independent actors. Subordinates
consider at least the following two factors when deciding when to exercise initiative:

    - Whether the benefits of the action outweigh the risk of desynchronizing the overall operation.

    - Whether the action will further the higher commander's intent.

1-81. The main consideration in exercising initiative is the urgency of the situation. If time permits,
subordinates attempt to communicate their new situation and recommended course of action to their
commander. Their commander then assesses the implications for the overall force, the impact on other
operations, and whether subordinates require additional support. However, subordinates must depart from
their orders when they are unable to contact their commander or when there is a limited amount of time to
seize a fleeting opportunity. If doubt exists about whether to contact their commander or depart from orders
and act to seize a fleeting opportunity, subordinates should act if they can do so within their commander's
intent.

1-82. Fostering a command climate that encourages disciplined initiative requires commanders to accept risk
and underwrite the good faith mistakes of subordinates in training, before the unit is committed to combat.
Commanders set conditions for subordinates to learn and gain the experience they need to operate on their
own. Subordinates learn to trust that they have the authority and responsibility to act, knowing their
commander will back their decisions. Because mutual trust and shared understanding constitute the
foundation of subordinate initiative, commanders train subordinates to act within the commander's intent in
uncertain situations.

###### **Command**


This chapter discusses the nature and elements of command. It then describes the role of the
commander in operations. The chapter concludes with a discussion of guides to effective
command.

**NATURE OF COMMAND**

2-1. Command is the authority that a commander in the armed forces lawfully exercises over subordinates
by virtue of rank or assignment. Command includes the authority and responsibility for effectively using
available resources and for planning the employment of, organizing, directing, coordinating, and controlling
military forces for the accomplishment of missions. It also includes responsibility for the health, welfare,
morale, and discipline of assigned personnel.

2-2. Command is personal. In Army regulations and doctrine, an individual is given the authority to
command, not an institution or group. The legal and ethical responsibilities of a commander exceed those of
any other leader of similar rank who is serving in a staff position. The commander alone is responsible for
what the command does or does not do.

2-3. Command is more art than science. It requires judgment and depends on actions only human beings can
perform. The art of command comprises the creative and skillful exercise of authority through timely decision
making and leadership. Commanders constantly use judgment gained from experience and training to
delegate authority, make decisions, determine the appropriate degree of control, and allocate resources.
Proficiency in the art of command stems from years of schooling, self-development, introspection, and
operational and training experiences. It also requires a deep understanding of the science of war.

2-4. The basic techniques of command do not change between echelons. However, direct leadership within
a command decreases as the level of command increases, and applying organizational leadership as described
in ADP 6-22 becomes more relevant. Officers prepare for higher command by developing and exercising
their skills when commanding at lower levels.

**ELEMENTS OF COMMAND**

2-5. The elements of command are authority, responsibility, decision making, and leadership. Authority is
the right and power to judge, act, or command. Commanders are responsible for the actions,
accomplishments, and failures of their subordinates, retaining overall accountability even when authority is
delegated. Decision making is the process of applying judgment to select a course of action most favorable
to accomplishing the mission. Leadership is the activity of influencing people by providing purpose,
direction, and motivation to accomplish the mission and improve the organization.

**AUTHORITY**

2-6. Authority is the right and power to judge, act, or command. Legal authority to enforce orders under the
law is a key aspect of command which distinguishes military commanders from staff officers and civilian
leaders. Commanders understand that operations affect, and are affected by, human interactions. As such,
they seek to establish personal authority. A commander's personal authority reinforces that commander's
legal authority. Personal authority ultimately arises from the actions of commanders and the resulting trust
and confidence generated by these actions. Commanders earn respect and trust by upholding laws, adhering
to the Army ethic, applying Army leadership principles, and demonstrating tactical and technical expertise.

2-7. Commanders may delegate authority to subordinates to accomplish a mission or assist in fulfilling their
responsibilities. This includes delegating authority to members of their staff. Delegation allows subordinates
to decide and act for their commander in specified areas. When delegating authority, commanders remain
accountable for mission accomplishment, for the lives and care of their Soldiers, and for effectively using
Army resources. Therefore, commanders use judgment in determining how much authority to delegate.

**RESPONSIBILITY**

2-8. Commanders are legally and ethically responsible for the decisions they make or do not make, and for
the actions, accomplishments, and failures of their subordinates. Commanders may delegate authority, but
they still retain overall accountability for the actions of their commands.

2-9. Command responsibilities include mission accomplishment; the health, welfare, morale, and discipline
of Soldiers; and the use and maintenance of resources. In most cases, these responsibilities do not conflict.
However, the responsibility for mission accomplishment sometimes conflicts with the responsibility for
Soldiers or the stewardship of resources. The importance of the mission informs commanders how much risk
to Soldiers and equipment to accept. When there is conflict among the three, mission accomplishment comes
before Soldiers, and Soldiers come before concerns for resources. Commanders try to keep such conflicts to
an absolute minimum.

**DECISION MAKING**

2-10. Commanders exercise their authority in part by making decisions and directing action. Decision making
involves applying both the art and science of war. Many aspects of military operations—movement rates,
fuels consumption, and weapons effects—can be reduced to numbers, calculations, and tables. They belong
to the science of war and are important to understanding what is possible with the resources available. Other
aspects—the impact of leadership, complexity of operations, and uncertainty about the enemy—belong to
the art of war. Successful commanders focus the most attention on those aspects belonging to the art of war.

2-11. For Army forces, decision making focuses on selecting a course of action that is most favorable to
accomplishing the mission. Decision making can be deliberate, using the military decision-making process
and a full staff, or decision making can be done very quickly by the commander alone. A commander's
decisions ultimately guide the actions of the force.

2-12. Decision making requires knowing if, when, and what to decide as well as understanding the
consequences of that decision. Critical to decision making is the ability to make decisions without perfect
information, knowing when enough information allows acceptable decisions, and the willingness to act on
imperfect information. Striking the balance between acting now with imperfect information and acting later
with better information is essential to the art of command. Effective decision making includes—

    - Developing and maintaining situational understanding.

    - Applying critical and creative thinking.

    - Determining the appropriate decision-making approach.

    - Applying judgment to make informed choices.

accurately. Commanders and staffs strive to maintain their situational understanding and work through

**Approaches To Decision Making**

2-23. Generally, there are two decision making approaches commanders use based on the situation. These
approaches are analytic and intuitive decision making. Analytic decision making provides commanders with
the most relevant information by employing systematic analysis of data. Through intuitive decision making,
commanders rely on their intuition to make decisions. Whether the commander relies on analytics or intuition,
the responsibility for the decision rests with them, not the analytic tools. Effective commanders consider their
own experiences, the experiences of their staff, and the available time and information when determining the
most suitable decision-making approach.

2-24. Commanders switch between and combine intuitive and analytical decision-making techniques as the
situation requires. Because uncertainty and the tempo of large-scale combat operations drive most decisions,
commanders emphasize intuitive decision making and develop their subordinates accordingly. However,
when time is available and depending on the operational context of a situation, commanders and staffs use
the military decision-making process or Army design methodology during planning.


**_Analytic Decision Making_**

2-25. Analytic decision making generates several solutions, compares those solutions to a set of criteria, and
selects the best course of action. It aims to produce the optimal solution by comparing multiple options. It
emphasizes analytic reasoning guided by experience, and commanders use it when time is available. This
approach offers several advantages. Analytic decision making—

    - Allows the methodical breakdown of tasks into recognizable elements.

    - Ensures commanders consider, analyze, and evaluate relevant factors and employ techniques such
as war-gaming.

    - Provides a systematic approach when a decision involves processing large amounts of data and
information.

    - Gives inexperienced personnel a logically structured approach.

    - Focuses on assessing the situation more than on comparing multiple options.

experience to recognize an acceptable course of action. There is a difference between sound intuition and
uninformed assumptions. When using intuitive decision making, leaders are aware of their own biases and
the differences between their current operational environment and those they experienced in the past.

2-30. By emphasizing experienced judgment over deliberate analysis, commanders increase tempo and retain
flexibility to deal with uncertainty. The intuitive approach is consistent with the fact that there are no perfect
solutions to operational challenges. Even when making intuitive decisions, commanders may have time to

to ensure they are feasible, acceptable, and suitable.

2-31. In practice, the two approaches are rarely mutually exclusive. When time is not critical, commanders
use an analytical approach or incorporate analysis into their intuitive decisions. Commanders blend intuitive
and analytic decision making to remain objective and make timely, effective decisions. Commanders seek as
much analysis as possible within the time available. In situations requiring immediate decisions, this analysis
may be no more than the commander's own rapid mental development, analysis, and selection of a course of
action—which may appear completely intuitive. Combining both approaches best accounts for the many
factors that affect decisions. An approach that combines analytic and intuitive decision making is the rapid
decision making and synchronization process found in FM 5-0.

**_Prioritize Resources_**

2-43. Commanders allocate resources to accomplish the mission. Allocating resources requires judgment
because resources can be limited. Considerations for prioritizing resources include how to—

    - Accomplish the mission effectively.

    - Protect the lives of Soldiers.

    - Apply the principles of mass and economy of force.

    - Posture their force for subsequent operations.

2-44. The primary consideration for allocating resources is how their use contributes to effective mission
accomplishment. Commanders do not determine how to accomplish a mission based on conserving resources
or giving all subordinates an equal share. Instead, they allocate resources efficiently to ensure effectiveness.
The objective—to accomplish the mission—guides every element of operations. A plan that does not
accomplish the mission, regardless of how well it conserves resources, is not effective.

2-45. The next priority is to protect the lives of Soldiers. Commanders determine how to protect the lives of
Soldiers before considering how to conserve material resources. They use material resources generously to
save lives. If there are different but equally effective ways to accomplish the mission, a commander considers
ways which use fewer resources.

2-46. The third aspect of resource allocation is based on two of the principles of war—mass and economy of
force. The principle of mass means that commanders weight the main effort with the greatest possible combat
power to overwhelm an enemy force and ensure mission accomplishment. Economy of force refers to
allocating the minimum essential combat power to all supporting efforts. Supporting efforts typically receive
fewer resources than the main effort. Commanders accept risk in supporting efforts to give weight to the
main effort.

**Location of the Commander**

2-60. One of the fundamental dilemmas facing all commanders is where to position themselves during
operations. As far as conditions allow, commanders should be forward where they can be seen and heard to
best influence the main effort. Commanding forward allows commanders to assess the state of operations
face-to-face with their subordinates and achieve shared understanding. It allows them to gather as much
information as possible about actual combat conditions when making decisions. However, commanding
forward does not mean taking over a subordinate's responsibilities.

2-61. Commanders consider their position in relation to both the forces they command and the mission. Their
location can have important consequences, not only for the command but also for executing operations. The
C2 system helps commanders position themselves forward without losing access to available information
and analysis.

2-62. In larger tactical- and operational-level commands, C2 nodes are normally the focus of information
flow and planning. However, commanders cannot always visualize the battlefield to direct and synchronize
operations. Commanders often assess the situation up front, face-to-face with subordinate commanders and
their units. Commanders employ their C2 system to position themselves wherever they can best command
without losing the situational understanding that enables them to respond to opportunities and changing
circumstances. The C2 system allows commanders to obtain the information they need to assess operations
and risks, and make necessary adjustments, from anywhere in an area of operations.

2-63. Commanders might not always be where the critical action is occurring. This probability reinforces the
necessity of training subordinates to operate using the mission command approach. Commanders then rely

**UNDERSTAND**

2-70. To understand something is to grasp its nature and significance. Understanding includes establishing
context—the set of circumstances that surround a particular event or situation. Understanding an operational
environment and its associated problems is fundamental to establishing a situation's context and visualizing
operations. An _operational environment_ is the aggregate of the conditions, circumstances, and influences that
affect the employment of capabilities and bear on the decisions of the commander (JP 3-0). For Army forces,
an operational environment includes portions of each of the five domains (land, maritime, air, space, and
cyberspace) understood through three dimensions (human, physical, and information). (Refer to FM 3-0 for
a detailed discussion on an operational environment.)

2-71. Part of understanding an operational environment includes identifying and understanding problems. In
the context of operations, an operational problem is a discrepancy between the current state of an operational
environment and the desired end state. An operational problem includes those issues that impede commanders
from accomplishing missions, achieving objectives, and attaining the desired end state.

2-72. Commanders collaborate with their staff, other commanders, and unified action partners to build a
shared understanding of their operational environment and associated problems. Planning, intelligence
preparation of the operational environment, and running estimates help commanders develop an initial
understanding of their operational environment. Commanders direct reconnaissance and develop the situation
to improve their understanding. Commanders circulate within the area of operations as often as possible,
collaborating with subordinate commanders and speaking with Soldiers. Using personal observations and
input from others (including running estimates from their staff), commanders improve their understanding of
their operational environment throughout the operations process. Ideally, perfect understanding is the basis
for decisions. However, commanders realize that uncertainty and time preclude achieving perfect
understanding before deciding and acting.

**NATURE OF CONTROL**

3-1. Command alone is not sufficient to translate vision into action and assure mission accomplishment.
Commanders also exercise control to direct and adjust operations as conditions dictate. Control is the
regulation of forces and warfighting functions to accomplish the mission in accordance with the commander's
intent. Determining the appropriate level of control, including delegating authorities and determining how
much decentralized execution to employ, is an essential aspect of mission command. (See Chapter 1 for a
discussion on levels of control.)

3-2. Control allows commanders to monitor and receive feedback regarding the situation during operations.
Based on this information, commanders modify their visualization and direct changes to an operation as
necessary. In the broadest terms, control helps commanders answer two questions:

    - What is the actual situation compared with the desired end state?

    - Are adjustments to the plan necessary to reconcile the situation with the desired end state?

3-3. Unlike aspects of command, which remain relatively similar among echelons, control functions increase
in complexity at each higher echelon. Control extends over the entire force and may include control of the
airspace over an assigned area below the coordinating altitude. (Refer to FM 3-52 for more discussion of
airspace control.)

3-4. Control is more science than art. It relies on objectivity, facts, empirical methods, and analysis. The
science of control supports the art of command. Commanders and staffs use the science of control to
understand the physical and procedural constraints under which units operate. Units are bound by such factors
as movement rates, fuel consumption, weapons effects, rules of engagement, and other legal considerations.
Commanders and staffs strive to understand aspects of operations they can analyze and measure, such as the
physical capabilities and limitations of friendly and enemy organizations.

3-5. Control requires a realistic appreciation of time and distance factors. This includes the time required to
initiate, complete, and assess directed actions. There is art in anticipating likely points of friction and factors
beyond subordinate control that invariably lead to delays during execution. The higher the echelon and the
larger the formation, the longer it takes to complete assigned tasks. This in turn requires an earlier decision
to achieve a desired effect or end state. The planning necessary to facilitate adequate control incorporates
time tolerances that account for the friction inherent in operations.

3-6. One strength of mission command is that it provides a measure of self-regulation during the conduct of
operations. Under mission command, control tends to be decentralized and flexible whenever possible in the
context of operations. Orders and plans rely on subordinates' abilities to coordinate among themselves to
create shared understanding and synchronize operations. By delegating decision-making authority to
facilitate decentralized execution, mission command increases tempo by improving a subordinate's ability to
act in rapidly changing situations. As a result, the mission command approach to C2 is inherently less
vulnerable to disrupted communications.

organizing their C2 system to meet the requirements of the situation. The elements of control are—

**Data Analytics**

3-14. **_Data analytics_** **is the process of transforming sets of data to identify patterns, trends, and insights** .
It is a multidisciplinary field of study that encompasses data analysis, data science, and data engineering.
Data analytics employs a wide range of analysis techniques (such as math and statistics) and technologies
(such as artificial intelligence and machine learning).

3-15. Data analytics enhances the identification of relevant information by processing large volumes of data
to identify patterns, trends, and relationships aligned to CCIRs. It helps commanders and staffs focus on data
that directly supports decision points, mission objectives, and critical information requirements. Data
analytics can transform unprocessed data into clear, actionable information.

3-16. For Army leaders, data literacy begins by understanding the four levels of data analysis and how each
contributes to situational understanding through the cognitive hierarchy. Each of the four data analytics levels
(descriptive, diagnostic, predictive, and prescriptive) involves a deeper and more complex analysis with data.
As an organization moves from descriptive to prescriptive analysis, the analytical techniques become more
technically sophisticated. At the same time, potential insights become increasingly useful to decision makers.


**_Note._** The Army recruits, trains, and educates Soldiers and Army civilian professionals to serve as
data analysts, data engineers, software developers, and data scientists in the operational and
institutional force. These high-demand and low-density specialists are normally assigned at
echelons above brigade in the operational force. Lower echelons benefit from the analysis and
products developed at higher echelons.


**_Descriptive Analytics_**

3-17. Descriptive analytics is the foundational level of data analytics, focusing on summarizing historical
data to understand what has happened and when it happened. The goal is to provide a clear picture of
historical data and trends. This level of analytics involves data aggregation and mining techniques to provide
insight into past events and performance. It includes statistical measures like mean, median, and mode and
data visualization tools like charts and graphs. Descriptive analytics answers questions such as “What
happened?” and “How many?” Descriptive analytics in a military context can be compared with the first
three steps of intelligence preparation of the operational environment (define the operational environment,
describe the environmental effects on operations, evaluate the threat).


**_Diagnostic Analytics_**

3-18. Diagnostic analytics explains why something happened by identifying patterns and correlations within
the data. It uses techniques like correlation analysis to find relationships between variables and data mining
to uncover hidden patterns within large datasets. By identifying these connections and root causes, diagnostic
analytics seeks to answer questions such as, “Why did this happen?” and “What are the underlying reasons?”
This process helps to understand the underlying reasons for past outcomes.


**_Predictive Analytics_**

3-19. Predictive analytics uses historical data and statistical algorithms to forecast future events. The goal is
to predict what is likely to happen based on patterns observed in the past. It provides insights into future
events by analyzing historical data and identifying trends. Techniques include regression analysis, statistical
modeling, time series analysis, and machine learning algorithms. Predictive analytics answers questions such
as “What is likely to happen in the future?” and “What trends can we expect?” Predictive analytics in a
military context can be compared to step four of intelligence preparation of the operational environment
(determine threat courses of action).

**_Prescriptive Analytics_**

3-20. Prescriptive analytics suggests actions that can affect desired outcomes. It predicts what will happen
and provides recommendations for facing future scenarios. Prescriptive analysis suggests actionable
recommendations and strategies based on predictive insights. It answers questions like “What should we do?”
and “How can we make it happen?” The purpose of prescriptive analytics is to determine the best course of
action. This last level involves complex algorithms and advanced tools, such as simulation, to advise on
possible outcomes and their impacts.


**_Note._** Data analytics can produce powerful insights. However, they are no substitute for the
leader's judgment. Data analytics can provide evidence for a decision, but it is up to leaders to
judge the strength of that evidence. Although commanders and other decision makers do not need

understand their capabilities, limitations, and implications.

**Direction and Feedback**

3-32. Commanders, through their C2 system, direct subordinates by establishing objectives, assigning tasks,
and providing instruction on how forces will cooperate to accomplish the mission. (See Chapter 4 for more
information on the C2 system). The primary means for communicating directions include plans and orders.
Mission orders focus subordinates on what to do and why to do it without prescribing exactly how to do it.
(See Chapter 1 for more information on mission orders). Other tools for providing direction include execution
matrices, the decision support template, and control measures.

3-33. Commanders use feedback to compare the actual situation with the plan and then decide if the plan
requires any changes or adjustments. Feedback comes from many sources: subordinates; higher headquarters;
or adjacent, supporting, and supported forces. It arrives continuously: before, during, and after operations.
Feedback helps commanders and subordinates gain shared understanding. For feedback to be effective, it
identifies any differences between the desired end state and the current situation. New information that
conflicts with the expectations established during planning requires commanders and staffs to validate those
expectations or revise them to reflect reality. This contributes to an accurate situational understanding that
allows commanders to exploit fleeting opportunities, respond to developing situations, modify plans, or
reallocate resources.

3-34. Feedback does not flow only from lower to higher headquarters; it also flows from higher to lower
headquarters. Normally, information from higher echelons to lower echelon headquarters consists of
information to adjust the subordinates' resources, plans, or missions. Multidirectional communication
produces shared understanding between higher commanders and subordinate forces that supports exercise of
mission command.

3-35. Effective commanders seek feedback from subordinates who are comfortable providing both positive
and negative reports. Commanders whose command climates make subordinates reluctant to share bad news
are likely to be poorly informed and operate from faulty assumptions that put operations at risk.

**ALLOW SUBORDINATES MAXIMUM FREEDOM OF DECISION AND ACTION**

3-54. Effective commanders impose minimum constraints on subordinates to enable freedom of action while

among subordinate and supporting forces. Commanders monitor this coordination and allocate available

3-55. In most instances, lower echelon commanders have the clearest understanding of their own situations.

commanders, is critical for effective decentralized execution. Commanders emphasize lateral coordination at

3-56. Commanders avoid establishing excessive limits on their subordinates' freedom of action. These limits
may come in the form of overly detailed orders that inhibit initiative and force subordinates to refer large
numbers of decisions to their higher headquarters. Excessive detail may be a result of emphasizing process
or procedure rather than an outcome—directing things already addressed in doctrine or standard operating
procedures. Commanders consider these items when deciding how to exercise control:

    - Limit control measures to those necessary to effect essential coordination.

    - Limit information requirements to the minimum needed to facilitate decision making.

    - Give subordinates as much leeway for initiative as possible.

3-66. Control allows organizations to respond to change, whether due to threat or friendly actions or

subordinates to recognize and respond effectively to emerging conditions and to correct for the effects of fog

3-67. Control supports flexibility and adaptability in two ways. First, it identifies the need to change the plan.
It does this through anticipating or forecasting possible adversary actions and by identifying unexpected
variances (opportunities, threats, or risks) from the plan. This occurs throughout the operations process.
Second, control helps commanders develop and implement options to respond to these changes in a timely
manner. Flexibility and adaptability provided by the appropriate level of control reduce an adversary's
available options while maintaining or expanding friendly options. Effective control provides for timely
action before adversaries can accomplish their objectives, allowing for the modification of plans as the
situation changes.

3-68. Instead of rigidly adhering to the plan, control focuses on information about emerging conditions. The
mission command approach to control provides flexibility by—

    - Allowing friendly forces to rapidly change their tasks, their task organization, or their plan in
response to changing circumstances.

    - Producing information about options to respond to changing conditions.

    - Communicating the commander's decisions quickly and accurately.

    - Providing rapid reframing when the plan changes during execution.

    - Allowing collaborative planning and execution to respond to the progress of operations.

4-5. Within C2, control is the regulation of forces and warfighting functions to accomplish the mission in
accordance with the commander's intent. Commanders control operations by communicating information to
synchronize and direct the actions of subordinate forces. Staffs aid commanders in exercising control by
supporting the commander's decision making; assisting subordinate commanders, staffs, and units; and
keeping units and organizations outside the headquarters informed. (See Chapter 3 for more details on
control.)

**ORGANIZE AND EMPLOY THE COMMAND AND CONTROL SYSTEM**

4-6. To achieve effective C2, commanders organize and employ the C2 system. Commanders organize their
C2 system to support decision making, maintain relevant information, and communicate directives. Once the
C2 system is organized, commanders then employ it through the operations process, integrating and
synchronizing warfighting functions across multiple domains. Together, these create a framework for
effective C2.


**Organize**

4-7. Commanders organize the personnel within their C2 system to assist them and exercise control on their
behalf. They establish clear command relationships and assign responsibilities to empower staff members
and subordinate commanders to make decisions at the lowest level. Commanders position key personnel,
such as liaisons, to maintain relevant information and promote a shared understanding across the command.
Commanders delegate authority to the appropriate individuals, including seconds in command and command
sergeants major, to ensure timely decision making and enhance operational effectiveness.

4-8. In addition to organizing people, commanders organize their processes and networks to ensure effective
C2. Processes, such as the operations process and integrating processes, structure the activities necessary to
support decision making and issue directives. These processes are supported by networks designed to share
data that meet information requirements and help create and maintain situational understanding. These
networks are accessed through C2 nodes, facilitating the flow of relevant information throughout the
organization.

4-9. The final component of the C2 system that commanders organize is C2 nodes. Commanders integrate
people, processes, and networks into C2 nodes, which serve as physical and virtual locations for the staff to
assist the commander in maintaining visualization and issuing directives. While the configuration of these
C2 nodes may vary, it is primarily determined by the missions assigned and the operating environment.


**Employ**

4-10. The Army's framework for employing C2 is the operations process. The operations process puts the C2
system into action. Commanders, staffs, and subordinate units employ the operations process to integrate and
synchronize the warfighting functions across multiple domains and synchronize forces to accomplish
missions. This includes integrating processes such as intelligence preparation of the operational environment
and the targeting process. The unit's battle rhythm serves as a structured approach to coordinate and
synchronize major activities of the operations process.

4-11. The unit's battle rhythm synchronizes the various processes and activities that occur within the
operations process. (Refer to FM 6-0 for details on developing the unit's battle rhythm.) Several disciplines
help improve the operations process and decision making. This includes critical and creative thinking, data
orchestration, data analytics, and knowledge management. (See Chapter 2 for a discussion of critical and
creative thinking and Chapter 3 for a discussion of data orchestration, data analytics, and knowledge
management.)

**Commanders**

4-17. Commanders are responsible for the actions of their subordinates. Commanders may delegate authority,
but delegation does not absolve commanders of their responsibility to the higher echelon commander.
Commanders are always accountable for what happens, or fails to happen, within their command.

4-18. Where the commander locates within the assigned area, and at what time, are important considerations
for effective C2. No standard pattern or simple prescription exists for command presence; different
commanders lead differently. Commanders balance their time among the staff sections, subordinate
commanders, forces, and other organizations to make the greatest contribution to success. (See Chapter 2 for
more discussion on the location of the commander.)

4-19. C2 nodes serve as the focus for information exchange, planning, and analysis. They provide
commanders with direct access to staff members and allow them to communicate with superior, subordinate,
and supporting commanders. While at a C2 node, commanders provide vital face-to-face guidance to staff
members when developing plans and controlling operations. By moving to the locations of subordinates or
to critical points in an area of operations, commanders can better assess and influence the state of operations.
They personally gauge the condition of their units and leaders and consult directly with subordinate
commanders performing critical tasks. By being forward, commanders also motivate subordinates through
personal example.


**Seconds in Command**

4-20. At all levels, the second in command is the commander's principal assistant. The second in command
may be a deputy commander, an assistant commander, or the executive officer. Commanders determine the
duties and responsibilities of their deputy and assistant commanders. Usually, at company through brigade
echelons, the executive officer is the second in command. In a theater army, corps, or division, the deputy or
assistant commander extends the commander's span of control in areas and functions that the commander
designates.

4-21. Deputy or assistant commanders normally do not have staff. When they have specific responsibilities,
the headquarters staff assist them as their commander prescribes. Deputy or assistant commanders may give
orders to the staff within the authority the commander delegates to them. They may go to the chief of staff at
any time for staff assistance. If a deputy or assistant commander needs to form a team for a specific purpose,
the commander may form one from headquarters elements or subordinate units or make a subordinate unit's
headquarters available.

4-22. In organizations with more than one deputy or assistant, the commander designates which one is the
second in command. Delegating authority to the seconds in command reduces the burden on commanders
and allows them to focus on particular areas or concerns while their seconds in command concentrate on
others. Normally, commanders delegate authority to seconds in command to act in their name for specific
functions and responsibilities. A second in command has important responsibilities in the following
circumstances:

    - Temporary absence of the commander.

    - Succession of command.

    - Delegation of authority.

    - As deputies of joint and multinational forces.


**_Temporary Absence of the Commander_**

4-23. Officers who are second in the chain of command assume duties as delegated, either explicitly or by
standard operating procedures, when the commander is temporarily absent or resting. Lack of sleep can
impair judgment and creative thinking capabilities. A commander's sleep plan includes delegating authority
to the second in command during selected times to give the commander time to sleep. Commanders may also
be absent from the command temporarily. In this case, the second in command assumes command
temporarily and makes decisions that continue operations in accordance with the commander's intent and
policies.

**_Succession of Command_**

4-24. Commanders may be killed, wounded, medically incapacitated, or relieved of command. In these
situations, the second in command normally assumes command. At brigade and lower echelons, executive
officers or senior subordinate unit commanders normally assume command. At higher echelons, deputy or
assistant commanders may not be senior to subordinate unit commanders. In this case, the operations order
specifies succession of command, and the second in command exercises command until the designated
successor assumes command. However, commanders may designate a second in command who is junior to
subordinate commanders as their successor in command. (Refer to AR 600-20 for regulatory guidance.)

4-25. Seconds in command must be able to assume command at any time. They must stay informed of the
situation. Commanders inform their second in command of any changes to their visualization or intent. The
chief of staff keeps the second in command informed of staff actions. Commanders continually train their
seconds in command for command at their level.


**_Delegation of Authority_**

4-26. Delegating authority to the second in command reduces the burden of commanders' responsibilities. It
allows them to focus on particular areas or concerns while their seconds in command concentrate on others.
Normally, commanders delegate authority to their seconds in command to act in their name for specific fields
of interest and responsibility. Doing this decentralizes decision making while allowing the commander to
keep overall command.


**_As Deputies of Joint and Multinational Forces_**

4-27. When an Army headquarters serves as the headquarters of a joint or multinational force, appointing a
deputy commander from another service or a multinational partner is often appropriate. These deputy
commanders may also exercise command over forces of their Service or nation. They serve as important
advisers to the Army commander. They facilitate understanding among participating Service or national
forces. In this case, succession of command depends on joint and multinational doctrine, law, or international
agreement. However, the legal authorities of a multinational deputy may not be the same as a U.S. officer of
the same grade. Careful attention is paid to their authority to allocate resources, like Title 10 funds.


**Senior Noncommissioned Officers**

4-28. The command sergeant major is the senior noncommissioned officer of the command at battalion and
higher levels. The first sergeant and platoon sergeant are the senior noncommissioned officers at battery,
company, troop, and platoon levels. These senior noncommissioned officers carry out policies and enforce
standards for the performance, individual training, and conduct of enlisted Soldiers. They give advice and
initiate recommendations to commanders and staffs in matters pertaining to enlisted Soldiers. In operations,
commanders employ their command sergeants major and first sergeants throughout the area of operations to
extend command influence, assess morale of the force, and assist during critical events. Platoon sergeants
perform similar functions at the platoon level.

**_Characteristics of Good Staff Members_**

4-37. Good staff members understand how to effectively communicate with their commander, and they
discern what information is vital to their commander's ability to C2. They seek a shared understanding of the
operational environment with their commander and with the commanders of both higher and subordinate
headquarters. This shared understanding includes the commander’s visualization of the operational approach,
including the commander's intent. Good staff members—

    - Are competent. They are experts in doctrine and the processes and procedures associated with their
branch or functional area, as well as the operations process. They understand the duties of other staff
members enough to accomplish coordination both vertically and horizontally. Staff officers are data
literate to evaluate data sources for reliability.

    - Bring clarity. They clearly articulate and effectively present information orally, in writing, and
visually. They simplify problems in complex operational environments by explaining the meaning
of information and not simply providing raw data to the commander.

    - Exercise candor. They tell the commander what they believe, not what the commander wants to
hear. They are willing to tell the commander both good and bad news. Any staff work eventually
affects Soldiers, who execute staff officer recommendations approved by the commander. Staff
officers never forget that their recommendations affect Soldiers.

    - Exercise initiative. They anticipate requirements rather than waiting for instructions. They anticipate
what the commander needs to accomplish the mission and prepare answers to potential questions
before they are asked.

    - Apply critical and creative thinking. As critical thinkers, staff members discern truth in situations
where direct observation is insufficient, impossible, or impractical. They determine whether
adequate justification exists to accept conclusions as true. As creative thinkers, staff members look
at different options to solve problems. They use proven approaches (drawing from previous similar
circumstances) and innovative approaches (coming up with completely new ideas). Staff members
use creative thinking to apply imagination and depart from the old way of doing things.

    - Are adaptive. They recognize and adjust to changing conditions in the operational environment with
appropriate, flexible, and timely actions. They rapidly adjust and continuously assess plans, tactics,
techniques, and procedures.

    - Are flexible. They avoid becoming overwhelmed or frustrated by changing requirements and
priorities. Staff members remain flexible and adjust to any changes. They set priorities when there
are more tasks to accomplish than time allows. They learn to manage multiple commitments
simultaneously.

    - Possess discipline and self-confidence. They understand that all staff work serves the commander,
even if the commander rejects the resulting recommendation. Staff members do not give a “half
effort,” even if they think the commander will disagree with their recommendations. Alternative and
unpopular ideas or points of view assist commanders in making the best possible decisions.

    - Are team players. They cooperate with other staff members within and outside their headquarters.
This practice contributes to effective collaboration and coordination.


**_Staff Relationships_**

**COMMAND AND CONTROL NODES**

4-57. C2 nodes serve as locations from which commanders, supported by their staff, direct operations and
integrate and synchronize combat power to achieve missions across the spectrum of military operations.
Commanders organize the various components of the C2 system into these C2 nodes based on mission
requirements and the specific situation, enabling them to effectively exercise mission command. Planning
considerations for C2 node organization and employment can be categorized as—

    - Those contributing to effectiveness.

    - Those contributing to survivability.

4-58. In many cases, these two considerations work in opposition to one another. For example, concentrating
staff and systems in fewer, larger C2 nodes enhances collaboration and unity of effort but creates single
points of failure and a high payoff target for the enemy. Dispersing functions across many, smaller nodes
increase survivability by complicating enemy targeting but can introduce friction and reduce the speed of
decision making. For this reason, commanders analyze the mission and associated risks to find an acceptable
level of balance between the two.


**Effectiveness**

4-59. C2 nodes are arranged to facilitate coordination, exchange of information, and rapid decision making.
A C2 node communicates with higher echelon, subordinate, adjacent, supporting, and supported units and
moves as required. While any individual C2 node can be configured to fulfill any function, no single node
can perform all the functions simultaneously. Therefore, effective C2 relies on the integrated and distributed
execution of tasks across multiple nodes. The performance of any C2 node is assessed based on its ability to
execute the following tasks:

    - Building and maintaining situational understanding.

    - Conducting planning.

    - Conducting data orchestration, data analytics, and knowledge management.

    - Controlling operations.

    - Assessing operations.

    - Coordinating with internal and external organizations.

    - Performing C2 node administrative activities.


**_Building and Maintaining Situational Understanding_**

4-60. Building situational understanding to support C2 is a primary, collaborative function of all C2 nodes.
Staffs transform raw data into knowledge through activities like maintaining running estimates and battle
tracking. Technology is instrumental in this effort, enabling mission command systems like the COP to
rapidly disseminate information and create a shared understanding across the force. While these systems must
be resilient enough to perform in degraded environments, their primary role is to leverage information for a
decisive advantage. The resulting knowledge is provided as recommendations. This allows commanders to
apply judgment, visualize the operation, and make timely decisions to drive the mission.


**_Conducting Planning_**

4-61. Within a C2 node, planning is the primary process for turning situational understanding into a
synchronized course of action. The effectiveness of this process comes from the collaboration it creates,

which builds unity of effort among the staff and with subordinate units. This unity of effort is what makes a
C2 node effective, as it ensures all actions contribute to a common goal and provides a clear framework for
execution. Ultimately, this allows the entire force to operate with disciplined initiative, making the C2 node
the central point for driving agile and coherent operations.


**_Conducting Data Orchestration, Data Analytics, and Knowledge Management_**

4-62. Effective data orchestration, data analytics, and knowledge management are the engines that transform

patterns, assess operational progress, and produce actionable insights. Knowledge management provides the
overarching framework to share these insights across the staff, fueling tools like the COP and running
estimates. This disciplined process is what makes a C2 node effective, as it provides the commander with the
refined knowledge needed for timely decision making.


**_Controlling Operations_**

4-63. Controlling operations is the active process by which a C2 node directs, regulates, and synchronizes
the force during execution. This function makes a C2 node effective by translating current situational
understanding into concrete action, allowing commanders to adapt the plan as conditions change. The C2
node ensures the commander’s intent is being met and the force remains unified in its effort by managing
resources, deconflicting movements, and synchronizing assets.


**_Assessing Operations_**

4-64. Assessing operations is the continuous process of monitoring and evaluating the progress of an
operation against the plan, making it the critical feedback mechanism within a C2 node. This function makes
a C2 node effective by constantly updating situational understanding by analyzing current information
against measures of performance and measures of effectiveness. The recommendations derived from this
analysis inform the commander's decisions to either continue the plan, make execution decisions, or make
adjustment decisions, ensuring the operation continuously adapts to achieve its objectives.


**_Coordinating with Internal and External Organizations_**

4-65. Coordinating with internal and external organizations enables a C2 node to synchronize the broader
actions of the force and achieve unity of effort. This continuous interaction with higher and adjacent units—
as well as joint, interagency, and multinational partners—is vital throughout the operations process. It
expands situational understanding by incorporating diverse information, deconflicts objectives during
planning, and ensures mutual support during execution. An effective C2 node acts as a hub for this
coordination, allowing it to integrate capabilities beyond its own formation and ensuring its plan is nested
within the larger operational framework.

4-66. Performing C2 node administrative activities encompasses the essential internal tasks that sustain the
node, its systems, and its personnel. These activities—which include managing the battle rhythm,
maintaining network connectivity and power, and ensuring information systems are functional—are what
make a C2 node effective on a fundamental level. A node that excels at these administrative functions can
sustain a high operational tempo and ensure the uninterrupted flow of information required for planning,
coordinating, and controlling operations. While seemingly routine, this disciplined administration is a direct
and vital contributor to mission success that guarantees the resilience of the C2 node, ensuring its ability to
exercise C2 continuously.


**_Note._** Standard operating procedures, training, and evaluations consider if C2 nodes can deliver
these seven key tasks in both mounted and dismounted configurations and under the demands of
both offensive and defensive operations.

**<u>Chapter 4</u>**


**Survivability**

4-67. The enemy prioritizes friendly C2 nodes as high-value targets. This necessitates a deliberate
employment that minimizes detection, complicates enemy targeting, and preserves the ability to perform
critical functions under attack or in disrupted, degraded, intermittent, and low-bandwidth conditions.
Commanders design C2 nodes to be temporary and reconfigurable, anticipating detection and the need for
adaptability over time. This design emphasizes dispersion, redundancy, deception, and rapid reconstitution,
rather than relying on the permanence of any single node.

4-68. The enemy conducts systematic counter-C2 operations rather than occasional strikes against C2 nodes.
Therefore, ensuring the survivability of C2 nodes requires a comprehensive approach that considers factors
such as—

    - Size.

    - Positioning.

    - Dispersion.

    - Hardening.

    - Signature management.

    - Deception.

    - Mobility.

    - Redundancy.


**_Size_**

4-69. A C2 node's size affects its mobility and survivability. Large C2 nodes increase capacity and ease faceto-face coordination. Their size, however, makes them vulnerable to multiple types of detection and attacks.
Smaller C2 nodes offer significant advantages—including enhanced concealment, ease of displacement, and
a reduced signature—which collectively mitigate the risks associated with enemy targeting. However, C2
nodes that are too small cannot sustain continuous, high-tempo operations. Commanders identify where to
accept risk in terms of node size and manning, allocating resources to balance risk and effectiveness over
various phases of an operation.


**_Positioning_**

4-70. Positioning of a C2 node directly affects its ability to control operations and survive. Commanders
weigh the benefit of positioning a C2 node for maximum effectiveness versus the survivability risk to the C2
node. Selecting a location requires thorough analysis of the mission variables of mission, enemy, terrain and
weather, troops and support available, time available, civil considerations, and informational considerations.


**_Dispersion_**

4-71. Dispersion of C2 nodes involves separating these elements to enhance survivability and reduce
vulnerability to enemy targeting. By spreading out C2 nodes, units create a smaller target profile for enemy
sensors and weapon systems. This approach complicates enemy detection efforts and reduces both casualties
and lost equipment in the event of an attack. Computing and communication technologies enable the C2
system to maximize effectiveness while dispersing to remain survivable.


**_Hardening_**

4-72. C2 nodes are hardened to offset electromagnetic or high explosive effects. Electromagnetic hardening
is deliberate actions taken to protect personnel, facilities, and equipment. Physical or structural hardening is
protection from high explosive effects through the construction of facilities to increase the protection of
occupants. Occupying existing hardened facilities and use of engineer assets to harden facilities provide
short-term hardening for C2 nodes, especially when dispersion and mobility options are limited.

4-73. Positioning C2 nodes underground—in bunkers, basements, or tunnel systems—whenever mission
requirements and terrain allow offers physical protection and reduces visual signatures. However, it presents
unique challenges that are addressed in standard operating procedures. These include communications
latency, antenna emplacement, ventilation, power provisioning, electromagnetic isolation, and emergency

operations or when remaining in one location for extended periods.


**_Signature Management_**

4-74. Signature management demands continuous attention to physical and electromagnetic signatures.
Emission control plans outline conditional thresholds, authorize burst or directional transmission techniques,
and designate remote antenna placements to distance radiating elements from personnel and equipment.
Physical signature control measures—camouflage, noise and light discipline—and movement discipline
complement emission control measures and pay close attention to avoiding overhead detection.


**_Deception_**

4-75. Deception involves misleading enemy observers, focusing surveillance and targeting incorrectly and
shifting assets away from actual C2 nodes. To do this, C2 nodes primarily use tactical deception. Tactical
deception maximizes the use of deceptive displays, feints, demonstrations, ruses, and diversions to mislead
enemy collectors. Successful tactical deception allows the commander to gain an advantage over an enemy
force by obscuring C2 node vulnerabilities and enhancing defenses.


**_Mobility_**

4-76. C2 nodes deploy efficiently and move within the assigned area as the situation requires. Mobility
discipline is essential for enhancing the survivability and operational effectiveness of C2 nodes. C2 nodes
are repositioned with enough frequency using irregular movement schedules to hinder detection and
targeting.


**_Redundancy_**

4-77. Essential functions and critical data are intentionally replicated across multiple C2 nodes to ensure
operational continuity. This redundancy allows C2 nodes to continue operations in the event of C2 node
movements or losses due to battlefield conditions. In operations, personnel and equipment are lost or fail
under stress. Redundancy ensures that the loss or degradation of any single node does not impede a
commander's ability to exercise effective C2.

**GUIDES TO ORGANIZING FOR COMMAND AND CONTROL**

4-78. The C2 system consists of all the resources used to support C2. It enhances the commander's ability to
conduct operations. Commanders organize a C2 system to—

    - Support the commander's decision making.

    - Collect, create, and maintain relevant information.

    - Prepare products to support the commander's and leaders' understanding and visualization.

    - Prepare and communicate directives.

4-79. How commanders organize a C2 system can complicate or simplify execution. Organizational
decisions establish the chain of command (including command and support relationships) and task
organization. They influence where commanders obtain information, whom they rely on for advice, and how
they supervise execution of their decisions. Organizational decisions affect the structure of the flow of
recommendations to commanders. The organization establishes formal communication channels and
determines how commanders distribute information throughout their forces.

4-80. Organization provides sources of group identity for soldiers assigned to a command. A command
operates most effectively when soldiers consider themselves members of one or more groups characterized
by high levels of loyalty, cooperation, morale, and commitment. This supports mission command.

4-81. Information flows vertically within the chain of command, but the organization does not limit its flow
to the chain of command. Information also flows laterally among adjacent, supported, and supporting units.
Information flows informally and unofficially—between individuals according to personal relationships—
and within formal channels. Informal channels provide important redundancy.

**<u>Chapter 4</u>**


4-82. Commanders continually evaluate the organization of their C2 system and adapt to changing
circumstances. When organizing for C2, commanders consider several items. These include—

    - The chain of command.

    - Span of control.

    - Unit integrity.

    - Degraded environments.

**THE CHAIN OF COMMAND**

4-83. The _chain of command_ is the succession of commanding officers from a superior to a subordinate
through which command is exercised (JP 1, Volume 2). The commander at each level responds to orders
from a higher commander and, in turn, issues orders to subordinates. In this way, the chain of command fixes
responsibility and sources of authority at each echelon while distributing them broadly throughout the force.
Each commander has designated authority and responsibility in an assigned area or area of responsibility.
Command and support relationships specify the type and degree of authority one commander has over
another, and the type and degree of support one commander provides to another. Commanders also have
authority over resources, which they allocate to complete the mission.


**Command and Support Relationships**

4-84. Establishing clear command and support relationships is fundamental to organizing for operations.
These relationships prescribe clear responsibilities and authorities among subordinate and supporting units.
Some forces are given command or support relationships that limit their commander's authority to prescribe
additional relationships. Knowing the inherent responsibilities of each command and support relationship
allows commanders to establish clear responsibilities when organizing their forces. (Refer to FM 3-0 for
more information on command and support relationships.)

4-85. Commanders designate command and support relationships within their authority to support the
concept of operations. Task organization helps subordinate and supporting commanders understand their
roles in the operation and contribute to achieving the commander's intent. Command and support
relationships carry with them varying responsibilities to subordinate units by parent and gaining units.


**Allocating Resources**

4-86. Mission command requires commanders to have authority over, or access to, all resources required to
accomplish the mission. Accordingly, commanders organize resources and forces when making
organizational decisions. Resource organization does not violate unity of command and supports unity of
effort. This organization or allocation of resources has minimum restrictions on their use, permitting
subordinates to further reallocate or to employ them as the tactical situation requires.

**SPAN OF CONTROL**

4-87. Organization ensures reasonable span of control, which refers to the number of subordinates or
activities under the control of a single commander. An effective organization enables the commander and
subordinate commanders to command without information overload. The commander establishes their span
of control to exercise C2 under all circumstances, including in degraded environments. A commander's span
of control does not exceed that commander's capability to command effectively. The optimal number of
subordinates varies based on the situation; in fluid and rapidly changing environments, a commander can
effectively supervise fewer subordinate elements. Conversely, having a greater number of subordinates can
enhance flexibility and provide more options and combinations.

4-88. Narrowing the span of control—that is, lessening the number of immediate subordinates—deepens the
organization by adding layers of command. The more layers of command in an organization, the longer it
takes for information to move up or down the organization. Consequently, the organization may become
slower and less responsive. On the other hand, an effort to increase tempo by eliminating echelons of
command or flattening an organization requires widening the span of control. Commanders balance width
and depth, so that the C2 organization fits the situation.

**<u>The Command and Control Warfighting Function</u>**


**UNIT INTEGRITY**

4-89. Effective commanders are flexible; they task-organize forces to suit the situation. This includes creating
nonstandard, temporary teams or task forces. However, commanders reconcile the need for organizational
flexibility with the requirement to create shared understanding and mutual trust. These characteristics result
from familiarity and stable working relationships.

4-90. Commanders consider the impact on mission command when task-organizing forces. Whenever
possible, commanders task-organize based on standing headquarters and habitually associated elements.
When this is not feasible and organizations are formed from a wide variety of units, commanders allow time
for training and establishing functional working relationships and procedures.

4-91. Once a force is task-organized and committed, commanders do not change the task-organization during
operations unless the benefits clearly outweigh the disadvantages. Reorganizations cost time, momentum,
effort, and tempo. Commanders also consider logistics factors, as the time required to change task
organization may counter any organizational advantages.

**DEGRADED ENVIRONMENTS**

4-92. When organizing for C2, commanders consider the impact of degraded environments on the C2 system.
The C2 system may be degraded as the result of hostile actions to contest the use of the cyberspace and space
domains or due to the lack of resources to provide sufficient network coverage in an area of operations. The
degradation may not be technological. The use of electronic warfare, radiological or nuclear weapons, and
adverse weather may create physical conditions that cause interference in the electromagnetic spectrum and
degrade the C2 system. The challenges of communicating while wearing chemical or biological protective
masks and the corrosive effects of chemical agents and decontaminants on communications equipment may
also degrade C2. All of these may interfere with a commander's ability to exercise C2.

4-93. To mitigate this risk and successfully conduct operations in degraded environments, commanders
cannot become overly reliant on technological capabilities. Commanders ensure their personnel are trained
on analog and manual C2 processes and are comfortable operating in degraded environments. Understanding
the functions performed by automated systems is critical to understanding what functions are performed in
an analog environment. Commanders ensure standard operating procedures are in place that will help the C2
system maintain its ability to function. Personnel are trained and proficient in—

    - Continuous operations.

    - Maintaining the COP.

    - Manual information sharing.

    - Staff integration and crosstalk.

    - Manual running estimates.

    - C2 node battle drills.

4-94. One way to deal with degraded communications is through primary, alternate, contingency, and
emergency communication planning. This establishes the primary, alternate, contingency, and emergency
methods of communications, typically from higher echelons to lower echelons. Establishing this plan requires
care that an alternate or contingency method of communications does not rely on the primary. The key to a
good plan is to establish redundancy so that communications are always available. Most units will have two
primary, alternate, contingency, and emergency plans: one for communications to higher echelon
headquarters and one for subordinate units. A plan for a higher echelon headquarters will likely be established
by that headquarters.

4-95. Ultimately, the doctrinal solution to operating in degraded environments is mission command. Even
under severely degraded conditions, Army forces continue to make decisions and act in the absence of orders,
when existing orders no longer fit the situation, or when unforeseen opportunities arise.
